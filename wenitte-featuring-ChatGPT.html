# Objective Laws of Debate A proposed framework derived from How Large Language Models Handle Ambiguity, and What a Logical Language like FuturLang Suggests, integrating Paul Graham’s Pyramid of Disagreement and principles of structured, evidence-based engagement. ## Preamble Debate is a process for examining claims, resolving ambiguity, testing evidence and improving shared understanding. Its purpose is not necessarily to produce agreement, but to establish what can be supported, what remains uncertain and where disagreements genuinely exist. Drawing on the paper’s analysis of ambiguity, implicit interpretation, clarification and logical representation, these proposed laws establish a framework for making debate more precise, transparent, consistent and independently verifiable. The framework also draws on Paul Graham’s How to Disagree, particularly his Pyramid of Disagreement, which distinguishes engagement with an argument’s substance from attacks on the person presenting it. These are proposed normative rules for debate, not experimentally validated universal laws. They aim to make disagreement more rigorous without eliminating the human context, emotional information or freedom of expression that natural language provides. ## Part I — The 12 Objective Laws of Debate Derived from the paper's analysis of ambiguity and logical language, with Graham's disagreement hierarchy informing the rules of engagement. Law 1 ## The Law of Explicit Meaning Every claim must be interpreted according to its stated meaning, not an assumed intention. * Define key terms when they have multiple meanings. * Distinguish literal statements from implications, examples and rhetorical expressions. * Do not attribute a meaning to an opponent's statement without establishing that interpretation. Objective test: Can an independent reader identify the claim being debated without having to guess what the speaker meant? Foundation: Lexical and pragmatic ambiguity; Section 2 and Section 3.1 of the paper. Law 2 ## The Law of Clarification When ambiguity materially affects the argument, clarification must precede judgment. * Identify the specific ambiguity. * Present the plausible interpretations without misrepresenting them. * Ask the speaker to clarify when the available context cannot resolve the issue. * Do not demand clarification when the intended meaning is already sufficiently established. Objective test: Has the disputed meaning been clarified sufficiently to evaluate the claim? Foundation: Selective clarification and calibration; Section 3.4 and Section 4.3. Law 3 ## The Law of Proposition Separation Each independently testable claim must be distinguishable from the other claims supporting it. * Separate premises, conclusions, assumptions and predictions. * Do not treat several connected claims as one indivisible proposition. * Identify which specific proposition is accepted, rejected or left unresolved. Objective test: Can each disputed proposition be examined independently without changing the meaning of the others? Foundation: Logical representation, scope and quantifier ambiguity; Sections 2 and 4.1. Law 4 ## The Law of Evidentiary Accountability Every factual claim presented as established must be accompanied by evidence appropriate to that claim. * Distinguish observation, documented fact, inference, hypothesis and opinion. * Identify the source of evidence where possible. * Make the reasoning connecting the evidence to the conclusion explicit. * Acknowledge when evidence is insufficient or unavailable. Objective test: Can the evidence and reasoning supporting the claim be independently examined? Foundation: Counterargument, refutation and the proposed evaluation of accuracy in Section 5. Law 5 ## The Law of Interpretive Symmetry The same standards of interpretation and evidence must be applied to equivalent claims, regardless of who presents them. * Interpret opposing arguments with the same care as one's own. * Apply consistent standards when accepting or challenging evidence. * Do not selectively interpret ambiguous language to favor one position. * Apply the same burden of proof to equivalent propositions. Objective test: Would the same interpretation and evidentiary standard be used if the positions of the participants were reversed? Foundation: The paper's discussion of sycophantic resolution, silent commitment and consistency; Section 3.5. Law 6 ## The Law of Argument Fidelity A participant must address the argument actually presented before challenging an alternative interpretation of it. * Represent the opposing claim accurately. * Do not substitute a weaker or more extreme version of the argument. * Clearly identify when an objection concerns an implication, an assumption or a different related argument. * Allow the other participant to correct an inaccurate representation. Objective test: Does the counterargument address the original proposition or explicitly identify the different proposition it addresses? Foundation: Graham's distinction between counterargument and refutation, and the paper's concern with silent interpretation. ![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://paulgraham.com\&sz=32) Paul Graham Law 7 ## The Law of Central Relevance Debate must distinguish between a central claim and supporting details. * Identify the principal proposition under dispute. * Establish which supporting premises are necessary to that proposition. * Address the central claim when the purpose is to challenge the overall argument. * Do not present the correction of an incidental error as proof that the central argument is false. Objective test: Does the objection materially affect the conclusion or a premise on which it depends? Foundation: Graham's DH5 and DH6, refutation and refutation of the central point. ![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://paulgraham.com\&sz=32) Paul Graham Law 8 ## The Law of Logical Consistency Claims and reasoning must be evaluated for internal consistency across the debate. * Identify contradictions between stated propositions. * Distinguish genuine contradictions from differences in definitions, scope or context. * Apply the same logical relationships throughout the discussion. * When a position changes, explicitly identify the revision and its justification. Objective test: Can the participant's relevant claims coexist without contradiction under the definitions and assumptions provided? Foundation: Inconsistency and paraphrase stability; Sections 3.5 and 5. Law 9 ## The Law of Proportional Certainty The degree of certainty expressed must not exceed the strength of the available evidence. * Distinguish what is known from what is inferred. * State uncertainty when evidence permits more than one reasonable interpretation. * Avoid presenting a plausible explanation as an established fact. * Update conclusions when relevant new evidence changes the basis for them. Objective test: Is the stated level of confidence justified by the quality, quantity and limitations of the evidence? Foundation: Calibration, ambiguity recognition and selective clarification; Sections 3.2–3.4. Law 10 ## The Law of Personal Neutrality The validity of an argument must be assessed independently of personal attacks, social identity or emotional reactions to the speaker. * Address propositions and reasoning rather than personal characteristics. * Do not treat disagreement as evidence of hostility or ignorance. * Discuss credibility or conflicts of interest only when they are relevant to the evidence. * Separate criticism of tone from criticism of substance. Objective test: Does the objection identify a relevant problem with the argument, rather than merely with its presenter? Foundation: Graham's DH0–DH2 and the paper's analysis of pragmatic ambiguity. ![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://paulgraham.com\&sz=32) Paul Graham Law 11 ## The Law of Explicit Revision Participants must be permitted to revise, qualify or withdraw a claim when the reasoning or evidence warrants a change. * Acknowledge when a premise has been disproved or weakened. * Distinguish a correction from a change of subject. * Record what new evidence or reasoning motivated the revision. * Do not treat a justified revision as an admission that every related claim is false. Objective test: Is the change in position transparent, and is its justification traceable to the discussion? Foundation: The paper's proposed measures of consistency and accuracy across paraphrases and clarification cycles; Section 5. Law 12 ## The Law of Unresolved Outcomes A debate must be allowed to conclude with uncertainty or disagreement when the available evidence does not establish a shared conclusion. * Identify points of agreement and disagreement separately. * Record which questions have been answered and which remain open. * Do not confuse rhetorical success, confidence or repetition with proof. * Define what additional evidence or clarification could resolve an outstanding question. Objective test: Does the conclusion accurately reflect what the debate established, rather than what a participant wanted it to establish? Foundation: The paper's distinction between model competence and ambiguity reporting, and its proposed auditable hybrid interface; Sections 4.3, 5 and 7. ## Part II — The Pyramid of Debate Engagement Paul Graham's How to Disagree describes seven levels of disagreement, from name-calling to refuting the central point. The levels describe the form of disagreement, not whether the resulting argument is true. ![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://paulgraham.com\&sz=32) Paul Graham GRAHAM'S PYRAMID OF DISAGREEMENT DH6 Refuting the central point DH5 Refutation DH4 Counterargument DH3 Contradiction DH2 Responding to tone DH1 Ad hominem DH0 Name-calling Conceptual illustration of Graham's hierarchy, not a score of an individual or debate. | Level | Form of engagement | Objective requirement | | --- | --- | --- | | DH0 | Name-calling | Replace personal abuse with a relevant claim. | | DH1 | Ad hominem | Identify an argument-related issue rather than relying on personal characteristics. | | DH2 | Responding to tone | Address the substance of the statement. | | DH3 | Contradiction | State the opposing proposition clearly. | | DH4 | Counterargument | Add relevant reasoning or evidence. | | DH5 | Refutation | Identify a specific error in the argument and explain it. | | DH6 | Refuting the central point | Identify and challenge the principal claim or a necessary premise. | The pyramid can be used as a descriptive tool for reviewing debate contributions. However, a DH6 argument can still contain errors, and a simple contradiction can sometimes identify a genuine issue. The level alone cannot establish correctness. ## Part III — The Futurological Proof Protocol The attached paper proposes a hybrid interface in which natural-language input is translated into a structured, logical representation, underspecified elements are flagged, and clarification is requested before action. The following protocol adapts that concept to debate. 1. Claim registration State the proposition being examined in one clear sentence. Separate it from supporting evidence, assumptions and conclusions. 2. Ambiguity detection Identify unclear terms, referents, quantifiers, scope and pragmatic implications that could change the interpretation. 3. Interpretation confirmation Record the intended interpretation or explicitly preserve multiple plausible readings if clarification is unavailable. 4. Logical decomposition Represent the argument as premises, inferences and conclusions. Identify which premises are necessary to the conclusion. 5. Evidence examination Assess the sources, relevance, reliability and limitations of the evidence, distinguishing factual support from inference and opinion. 6. Structured challenge Present a counterargument or refutation that identifies the exact claim or premise being challenged. 7. Consistency and revision Test the argument against the definitions, assumptions and evidence already accepted. Record corrections and justified revisions. 8. Outcome declaration Document established findings, unresolved propositions, remaining uncertainty and what additional information would be needed. ## The formal structure of a debate claim A claim can be represented using the following conceptual structure: C=(P,D,A,E,I,U)C = (P, D, A, E, I, U)C=(P,D,A,E,I,U) * PPP: Proposition being asserted * DDD: Definitions and scope * AAA: Assumptions and premises * EEE: Evidence * III: Inference connecting the evidence and premises to the proposition * UUU: Uncertainty and unresolved information A debate contribution should make these elements explicit to the extent required for the claim to be assessed. This is a proposed framework, not a formal syntax taken from a published FuturLang specification. ## Part IV — Objective Evaluation Without Declaring a Winner The paper proposes assessing accuracy, consistency, appropriate clarification and user effort. Applying those dimensions to debate makes it possible to assess the process without assuming that rhetorical performance establishes truth. | Dimension | Review question | | --- | --- | | Clarity | Is the proposition sufficiently precise to evaluate? | | Interpretive fidelity | Has the opposing position been represented accurately? | | Evidence | Is the claim supported by relevant, inspectable evidence? | | Logical validity | Does the conclusion follow from the stated premises? | | Consistency | Are definitions and standards applied consistently? | | Calibration | Is uncertainty represented appropriately? | | Relevance | Does the objection address the actual proposition? | | Transparency | Are assumptions, revisions and limitations disclosed? | | Resolution | Are agreements and unresolved issues clearly recorded? | | Accessibility | Is the level of precision proportionate to the purpose and needs of the participants? | These dimensions should be reported separately rather than combined into a single score. A debate may be clear but poorly evidenced, logically structured but based on an unverified assumption, or evidence-rich but unresolved because key terms remain disputed. ## Part V — Rules of Engagement To make the laws practical in live discussions, participants can use the following shared commitments: * State before challenging: Restate the claim in terms the original speaker recognizes. * Clarify before inferring: Ask a question when an ambiguity could materially affect the argument. * Evidence before certainty: Distinguish established facts from interpretations and hypotheses. * Refute rather than ridicule: Direct criticism toward the reasoning, evidence or premises. * Challenge the relevant point: Explain whether an objection targets a central claim, supporting premise or peripheral detail. * Revise without penalty: Allow participants to correct an argument without treating every revision as a defeat. * Disclose uncertainty: Do not manufacture agreement or certainty where neither is justified. * Conclude transparently: Record what has been established, what remains contested and what could resolve the disagreement. These commitments adapt Graham's argument-focused disagreement hierarchy while incorporating the paper's central concern: ambiguity is often resolved silently, making it difficult to determine whether participants are actually addressing the same proposition. ## Conclusion The Objective Laws of Debate offer a procedural framework for making disagreement more explicit and auditable. Their central premise is that a debate cannot be assessed reliably until the propositions, interpretations, assumptions and evidence involved are sufficiently clear. FuturLang, as characterized conceptually in the attached paper, provides a lens for reducing ambiguity through structured representation. Graham's Pyramid of Disagreement provides a way to describe how participants engage with opposing arguments. Combined, they suggest a debate process in which meaning is clarified, claims are decomposed, evidence is examined, refutations are directed at relevant propositions and conclusions reflect the actual state of the evidence. The ultimate standard is not whether a participant wins, speaks most confidently or persuades the audience. It is whether the debate makes the reasoning, evidence, uncertainty and remaining disagreement more transparent than they were before. ### References 1.
